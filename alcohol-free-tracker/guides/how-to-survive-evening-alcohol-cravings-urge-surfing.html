How to Beat 5 PM Alcohol Cravings

How the Urge Surfing Technique Works

Published 2026-10-21 · 5 min read · 100% Private Sobriety & Recovery Series

For anyone cutting back or quitting drinking, the hardest hour of the day is often the same: between 5:00 PM and 7:00 PM.

You finish work, close your laptop, or walk through the front door. Suddenly, a familiar urge shows up: "Just one glass of wine to unwind," or "A cold beer would take the edge off today." Even if you woke up determined not to drink, by 5:30 PM your evening routine can feel like it is running on autopilot.

Understanding why this happens and practicing a mindfulness approach called Urge Surfing can help you ride out evening cravings without breaking your momentum.

Why Cravings Peak at 5 PM: The Habit Loop

Evening cravings are rarely a sign of weakness or lack of discipline. They are usually a learned habit cue built up over years:

  • Work-to-home transition: If you regularly paired logging off from work with pouring a drink, your brain expects that familiar signal at 5:00 PM.
  • Environmental cues: Walking past the wine rack, hearing ice clink, or opening the fridge can trigger an automatic craving before you even stop to think.
  • End-of-day fatigue: After a full day of decisions and stress, your energy and patience are lower in the late afternoon, making quick relief sound extra appealing.
The Golden Rule of Habit CravingsA craving is not an order you have to obey; it is a temporary sensation. Cravings usually rise and pass, and many people find they ease within minutes if they do not act on them.

What Is Urge Surfing? (Developed by Dr. Alan Marlatt)

Developed by addiction psychologist Dr. G. Alan Marlatt and colleagues, Urge Surfing is a mindfulness-based coping practice that treats cravings like ocean waves:

  1. You cannot stop waves from forming, but you can learn to stay balanced on the board until a wave passes.
  2. When people try to forcefully fight or suppress a thought ("I must not think about wine"), it often feels even louder.
  3. Instead of fighting the craving or giving in to it, you step back and observe the physical sensations with calm curiosity.

In a study of college student smokers (Bowen & Marlatt, 2009), students taught urge-surfing techniques smoked fewer cigarettes over the next 7 days than controls, even though the technique did not necessarily reduce the urges themselves right away. The key shift is learning that you can notice an urge without acting on it. Additional practical coping tools for alcohol urges are covered in NIAAA Rethinking Drinking.

A Simple Urge Surfing Routine (Step-by-Step)

Whenever you feel an evening craving rising, try this brief three-part reset:

Step 1: Locate the Sensation in Your Body

Notice where the urge shows up physically. Is it a tightness in your chest, dryness in your throat, or restlessness in your shoulders? Silently describe it without judgment: "There is tension in my chest. There is restlessness in my hands."

Step 2: Slow Your Breathing (Box Breathing)

Slow, steady breathing helps settle physical tension. Inhale slowly through your nose for 4 seconds, hold gently for 4 seconds, exhale smoothly through your mouth for 4 seconds, and pause for 4 seconds. Repeat for a few cycles until your shoulders relax.

Step 3: Watch the Wave Soften and Switch Activities

Notice how the urgency shifts once you pause instead of pouring automatically. Grab a tall glass of sparkling water with fresh lime or herbal tea, step into another room, and give yourself credit for riding out the moment.

Building a New Evening Habit

Every time you ride out an evening craving without drinking, you practice a calmer response to 5:00 PM stress. Over the first few weeks, that automatic evening pull often becomes quieter and easier to handle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently asked questions

How long does an alcohol craving actually last?

Cravings usually rise and pass, and many people find they ease within minutes if they do not act on them, though stronger urges can linger longer or return in waves. Stepping away from triggers and slowing your breath often helps the peak pass.

What is the science behind urge surfing?

Urge surfing was developed by psychologist Dr. G. Alan Marlatt. Research on urge surfing in smokers (Bowen & Marlatt, 2009) found that practicing non-judgmental awareness of urges helped people act on cravings less often, even when urges still occurred at first.

What should I drink at 5 PM instead of alcohol?

Cold sparkling water with fresh citrus (lime or grapefruit), tart cherry juice with club soda, or iced herbal tea with a splash of non-alcoholic bitters provide a refreshing evening ritual without alcohol.
