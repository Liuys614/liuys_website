How to Get an Open Crumb in Sourdough Bread: Aeration, Hydration & Coil Fold Guide

Published 2026-11-07 · 6 min read · Levain Artisanal Baking Series
Quick Answer — How to Get an Open Crumb in Sourdough: To achieve a wild open crumb, combine high-protein bread flour (12.5%–13.5%) at 75%–78% hydration, develop extensibility through 3–4 gentle coil folds spaced 30 minutes apart, end bulk fermentation at 35%–45% volume rise (at 76°F–80°F), and shape minimally with wet hands to protect delicate internal air pockets.

Few milestones in artisanal bread baking are more coveted than slicing open a loaf to reveal an open wild crumb—a honeycomb lattice of glossy, translucent air cells surrounded by paper-thin gluten membranes. An open crumb is not merely decorative; it yields an exceptionally light, custardy mouthfeel that transforms homemade sourdough from heavy sandwich bread into world-class bakery quality.

However, getting a consistently open crumb is often misunderstood. Novice bakers frequently assume that drenching dough in 85%+ water is the only shortcut, only to end up with flat, gummy puddles. In reality, wild crumb structure is an engineering balance between flour protein quality, gluten extensibility, gentle coil handling, and stopping bulk fermentation at the exact biological endpoint (alongside your sourdough hydration percentage and bulk fermentation temperature chart).

The 4 Pillars of Open Crumb Architecture

1. Flour Selection & Gluten Extensibility (Not Just High Protein)

Protein percentage alone does not guarantee openness. You need a flour that balances elasticity (snap-back strength) with extensibility (the ability to stretch thin without tearing). A hard red spring wheat flour with 12.5% to 13.5% protein and moderate ash content (0.55%–0.60%) provides the ideal extensible gluten backbone. A 45-minute autolyse (mixing flour and water before adding active sourdough starter and salt) fully hydrates the protein chains, allowing cell walls to expand around gas pockets effortlessly.

2. The Hydration Sweet Spot: 75% to 78%

Water turns starch into steam during baking, expanding the gluten cavities. However, pushed beyond your flour's absorption capacity, excess water causes the dough matrix to liquefy and pool outward rather than upward. For 90% of home bakers, 75% to 78% hydration is the optimal threshold: wet enough to deliver steam-driven wild alveoli, yet structured enough to hold vertical tension in the oven.

3. Dough Strength via Gentle Coil Folds

Traditional aggressive stretch-and-folds pull dough upward forcefully, often degassing the delicate micro-cavities formed by wild yeast. In contrast, coil folds gently lift the dough from the center using wet fingers, allowing each side to fold softly underneath under its own weight. Performing 3 to 4 coil folds spaced 30 to 45 minutes apart organizes gluten sheets into stratified horizontal layers without popping early gas pockets.

4. Stopping Bulk Fermentation at the Perfect Window

The single greatest cause of a dense crumb is misjudging the bulk fermentation endpoint. Under-fermented dough yields fool's crumb (erratic giant voids atop a heavy, gummy base). Over-fermented dough degrades gluten enzymatically, producing a fragile, flat loaf that collapses. At 76°F–78°F (24°C–26°C), end bulk fermentation when the dough has risen by only 35% to 45%. The dough should feel pillowy, domed at the perimeter, with smooth subsurface blisters.

Why Wet Hands Beat Floured Surfaces for Open CrumbExcess raw flour worked into the dough during late bulk folds creates unfermented dry seams that prevent gas bubbles from expanding uniformly. Always moisten your fingertips with cold water during coil folds, and reserve bench flour exclusively for the final 60 seconds of preshaping.

Gentle Preshape & Tension Discipline

When turning your dough out onto the work surface, handle it like a fragile soufflé. Never punch down or flatten the round with your palms:

  1. Minimal Preshape: Use a metal bench knife to cup the dough in a single sweeping motion into a loose round. Rest 15–20 minutes uncovered to relax gluten tension.
  2. Envelope Stitch Shaping: Gently stretch the dough into a loose rectangle. Fold the lower third up, fold the sides inward without pressing down on the bubbles, and lightly roll into a cylinder.
  3. Cold Retard in Banneton: Chill the shaped dough in a rice-flour dusted banneton at 36°F–38°F (2°C–3°C) for 12 to 16 hours. Cold temperatures stiffen the outer skin, making scoring crisp and maximizing vertical oven spring when trapped steam vaporizes inside your Dutch oven.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the secret to an open crumb in sourdough bread?

The secret is balancing dough extensibility with fermentation timing. Use high-protein flour (12.5%–13.5%), keep hydration between 75%–78%, use gentle coil folds to preserve gas cells, and stop bulk fermentation at a 35%–45% rise so residual yeast energy fuels massive steam expansion in the oven.

Does higher hydration always produce a more open crumb?

No. Pushing hydration past your flour's capacity (e.g. 85%+) weakens gluten matrix integrity, causing dough to spread sideways and bake into a dense, gummy pancake. Most open-crumb loaves are baked at 75%–78% hydration with strong dough tension.

What is fool's crumb and how is it different from a wild open crumb?

Fool's crumb occurs when under-fermented dough enters the oven. Huge cavernous bubbles pool directly beneath the crust while the bottom and center remain dense, rubbery, and undercooked. A true wild open crumb features evenly dispersed alveoli throughout the entire cross-section.

Why are coil folds better than stretch and folds for open crumb sourdough?

Coil folds use gravity to tuck dough underneath itself gently without tensile tearing or popping gas pockets. This builds vertical structural layers while keeping internal carbon dioxide bubbles intact.
