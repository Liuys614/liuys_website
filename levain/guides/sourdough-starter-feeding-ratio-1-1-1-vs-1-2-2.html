Sourdough Starter Feeding Ratios: 1:1:1 vs. 1:2:2 vs. 1:5:5 Guide

Published 2026-11-04 · 6 min read · Levain Artisanal Baking Series
Quick Answer — Sourdough Starter Feeding Ratios: A 1:1:1 ratio peaks fastest (3–5 hours at 76°F / 24°C) for quick bake day prep; a 1:2:2 ratio peaks in 5–8 hours for standard same-day dough mixing; and a 1:5:5 or 1:10:10 ratio extends fermentation to 10–14 hours, perfect for overnight counter maintenance, sweet doughs, or diluting excess acetic acid.

Every sourdough baker eventually confronts a critical question before mixing dough: what feeding ratio should I use for my sourdough starter? While beginner recipes often instruct you to feed equal parts starter, flour, and water (a 1:1:1 ratio), experienced bakers manipulate feeding ratios like a precise clock to control fermentation speed, bacterial acidity, and yeast viability.

Feeding ratios are expressed in parts by weight (grams): Starter : Flour : Water. A 1:2:2 ratio means 1 part seed starter, 2 parts fresh flour, and 2 parts water (for example, 20g starter + 40g flour + 40g water). Changing this ratio directly alters the food supply available to your wild yeast (Candida humilis and Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and lactic acid bacteria (Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis), dictating exactly when your levain reaches maximum levitating power.

How Feeding Ratios Dictate Yeast Kinetics and Peak Timing

The time it takes for your sourdough starter to reach its peak—doubling or tripling in volume with a domed, bubbly surface—is directly proportional to the microbial dilution factor. In a 1:1:1 ratio, a massive population of active microorganisms is introduced to a relatively modest food supply. They consume available fermentable sugars (maltose, glucose, fructose) rapidly, leading to a quick rise and an equally rapid collapse.

When you shift to a 1:5:5 or 1:10:10 ratio, you are introducing a smaller microbial seed into a vast ocean of fresh starch and water. The yeast cells must undergo several rounds of cellular replication before their collective carbon dioxide output can inflate the gluten matrix. This biological lag phase stretches peak timing safely over 10 to 14 hours without exhausting yeast energy reserves.

Managing Acidity: Acetic Acid vs. Lactic Acid Balance

One of the most overlooked benefits of higher feeding ratios (such as 1:4:4 or 1:5:5) is flavor management. Starters fed repeatedly at 1:1:1 tend to become increasingly acidic over time. As pH drops below 3.8, acetic acid (vinegar flavor) dominates, which can weaken gluten integrity and degrade dough extensibility during your sourdough bulk fermentation.

Higher feeding ratios dilute residual acids, encouraging lactic acid production (creamy, yogurt-like notes) over harsh acetic acid. If your loaves have been tearing during shaping or producing a gummy, pale crumb despite proper sourdough hydration, a series of high-ratio 1:5:5 feedings will reset starter health and restore gluten resilience.

Temperature vs. Ratio: The Two Levers of Starter Scheduling

Your starter does not live in a vacuum; ambient kitchen temperature acts as a thermal amplifier on your chosen feeding ratio:

  • Winter / Cold Kitchens (64°F–68°F / 18°C–20°C): Fermentation slows dramatically. Use a lower ratio like 1:1:1 or 1:2:2 with warm water (85°F / 29°C) to achieve a reasonable 6-hour peak.
  • Ideal Baker's Zone (74°F–78°F / 23°C–26°C): Standard ratios perform predictably. Use 1:2:2 for daytime baking or 1:5:5 for overnight bakes.
  • Summer / Warm Kitchens (80°F–86°F / 27°C–30°C): High temperatures hyper-accelerate enzymatic breakdown. A 1:1:1 feed will peak and spoil within 2.5 hours. Step up to 1:5:5 or 1:10:10 with cold tap water to keep your starter from over-fermenting overnight.

How to Identify True Starter Peak (Sensory Cues)

Never bake by clock time alone. Always confirm your starter has reached true peak before mixing it into flour:

  1. The Domed Surface: When actively rising, the top of your starter curves upward in a convex dome. Once the center flattens or begins dipping slightly concave, peak has just passed.
  2. Aerated Webbing: Looking through clear glass, the entire jar should display a honeycomb network of gas tunnels rather than dense paste.
  3. Sweet Yeasty Aroma: Peak starter smells fruity, boozy, and pleasantly yeasty—never purely acetone or harsh nail-polish remover.
  4. The Float Test: Drop a teaspoon of starter gently into room-temperature water. If it floats buoyantly, the gluten web holds enough trapped CO₂ for bread dough.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I use my starter before or after it peaks?

Using a starter before its peak means wild yeast populations have not multiplied sufficiently, leading to sluggish bulk fermentation and dense bread. Using a starter long past its peak introduces high enzymatic acidity, which breaks down the dough's gluten matrix, resulting in sticky dough that spreads flat in the oven.

How often should I feed my sourdough starter if stored in the refrigerator?

A healthy, mature starter stored in the refrigerator (38°F / 3°C) needs to be fed once every 7 to 10 days. Feed it at a 1:2:2 or 1:3:3 ratio, allow it to rise at room temperature for 1 to 2 hours until fermentation begins, and then place it in the fridge.

Why does a 1:5:5 feeding ratio take longer to peak?

In a 1:5:5 feeding ratio, a small starter inoculum is given five times its weight in flour and water. The wild yeast and bacteria take several hours longer to consume the available starches and produce enough CO₂ to double in volume, extending peak time to 10–12 hours.

Should I use warm or cool water when feeding my sourdough starter?

Use water between 78°F and 82°F (25°C–28°C) if your kitchen is cool or if you need the starter to peak faster. In a warm summer kitchen (80°F+ / 27°C+), use cool or cold water (65°F–68°F / 18°C–20°C) to prevent rapid over-fermentation.
